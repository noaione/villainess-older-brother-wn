# Chapter 3: A Distorted Power Dynamic

June. It’s been about two months since the “Tournament System” was introduced.
The system seems to have finally taken root among the students. First-years are
still recklessly challenging each other without pause, but even the
upperclassmen are engaging in “tournaments” a few times a day.

As expected, not many dare to pick a fight with us. Even the most reckless
first-years lack the guts to challenge those with epithets, so neither I, Caron,
Minerva, nor Nina have fought in a single “tournament.”

On the other hand, Orca, Marina, and Skia have been battling frequently.
Especially the latter two, who are challenged often. One’s a commoner, and the
other couldn’t use magic until recently—those labels are likely the cause. Well,
it’s not as bad as it could be, compared to others.

From what I’ve observed over the past month, most students are participating in
“tournaments” about once a week. Dan, Miria, and Yurika, who are close to me,
fit this pattern. They’ve been winning their matches, but they’re challenged far
more often than most. Even with us Foranadas acting as a deterrent, it doesn’t
stop entirely. Without the rule allowing match refusals and the safety barriers,
they might’ve collapsed from exhaustion.

However, the biggest issue this time isn’t the sheer number of participants.

“The first-years are acting a bit strange,” the Principal said, her face
contorted into a grimace.

At first glance, she appears to be a beautiful girl of about ten, with glossy,
long black hair. But in truth, she’s the “Witch of Life,” a figure feared for
centuries—a loli-babaa who’s easily hundreds of years old.

At her summons, I was seated on the sofa in the Principal’s office. The phrase
“alone with a beautiful girl” might sound glamorous, but the reality was a
heavy, oppressive atmosphere dominating the room.

“This year’s top first-years are too strong,” she continued. “Aside from you
Foranadas, it’s abnormal for there to be double-digit numbers of first-years who
can effortlessly defeat upperclassmen.”

As she said, numerous upperclassmen have been defeated by a handful of
first-years, and not just defeated—utterly overwhelmed, sometimes in mere
moments. If it were Tara or another Foranada, I could understand. But the ones
racking up consecutive victories are completely unrelated students. And there
are nearly thirty of them. It’s no wonder the Principal is clutching her head in
frustration.

Still, there’s one thing I need to ask.

“Why was I called here?” I said, leaning forward slightly.

I could follow the flow of the conversation—she wants to investigate this
anomaly among the first-years. But I don’t see why _I_ was summoned. I have no
motive or benefit in getting involved. At the end of the day, this is an issue
among the first-years, and we have no direct connection to it. The only link is
Tara, and I’ve already warned her not to accept “tournament” challenges
recklessly, so I’m even less inclined to intervene.

If anything, the ones who developed the magical tools and the operational system
for the “Tournament System” are us, but this issue doesn’t seem related to that.
If anyone should be called, it’s the student council who planned it. Someone
like Arianorth, with her sharp intellect, would be perfect for this.

So why skip all that—or maybe she’s already consulted them—and call _me_? That’s
what I don’t get.

At my question, the Principal tilted her head curiously, her expression almost
childlike. If you isolated this moment, she’d pass for an innocent girl, but
given the situation, all it did was spark a faint irritation in me. What’s with
the clueless act?

I tried again, my tone sharper. “Why call me about the first-years? Isn’t this
something the academy or the student council should handle? Just to be clear,
I’ve kept my promise from before. This incident has nothing to do with me.”

I’m referring to our prior agreement: if there’s a situation involving students
tied to my precognitive knowledge of the original story, I’m to be informed in
advance. I haven’t broken that deal. This abnormal strength among the
first-years wasn’t mentioned in the original story.

“Ah, aah\!” the Principal exclaimed, finally grasping my point.

She nodded awkwardly, her movements betraying a nervous agitation as she sank
into thought, visibly flustered.

I watched her with suspicion but waited for her to calm down.

Yet, no matter how long I waited, her agitation didn’t subside.

Now I was starting to worry. Normally, she’d say something like, “This could
spread to other years. It’s easier to crush the spark while it’s small,” or
dangle some benefit tied to her authority.

Some might think, _If you knew it’d come to this, why not just agree from the
start?_ But blindly complying every time affects the dignity of an earl. I have
to negotiate to some degree, or it could lead to troublesome situations later.
That’s why I went through the hassle.

The Principal should understand this, which makes her pure bewilderment all the
stranger.

“You okay? Feeling off or something?” I asked, rising slightly from the sofa and
leaning toward her to check her complexion.

Startled by my approach—she hadn’t noticed me move—she let out a shrill “Hya\?!”
and practically jumped.

“N-n-n-no, I’m perfectly fine\!” she stammered. “I just... made a rather
unbelievable discovery, and it threw me off. That’s all\! Nothing’s wrong\!”

“You’re freaking out like crazy,” I said, my tone slipping into casual bluntness
at her textbook-level panic.

Her eyes darted wildly, her cheeks faintly flushed, but she pressed on. “Th-the
issue with the first-years could spread to other years over time. It’s easier to
nip the problem in the bud while it’s young, right? B-but, I admit, there’s
little reason to drag an earl like you into this. I-I promise, within the scope
of my authority, I’ll grant whatever convenience you desire\!”

She rattled off the essentials in a rapid, almost desperate torrent, as if she
couldn’t wait to end this meeting.

...Huh? Wait a second. For just a moment, I caught a familiar emotion from her—

“Any objections\?!” she shouted, cutting off my attempt to pinpoint it.

We’re in the middle of negotiations; I can’t afford to ignore that. I refocused
and met her gaze. “About that ‘convenience’—can we discuss it later? I can’t
think of anything off the top of my head.”

Foranada can handle most things on our own, so there’s little I’d need to ask of
her. Maybe something academy-related?

She visibly relaxed, relief washing over her. “That’s fine. It’s my selfish
request, after all. I’ll do my best to meet your demands.”

“That’s generous,” I said, raising an eyebrow.

“It’s for the students,” she replied firmly.

She’s unshakable in that regard. Her wholehearted dedication to her students
never wavers. Perverted as she may be, she’s not a bad witch.

I stood up. “Alright, I’ll start looking into it.”

“Please do,” she said. “We’ll investigate on our end as well.”

“Contact me if you find anything.”

“Understood.”

By the end, she’d regained her composure, and we were able to converse normally.

I gave a brief farewell and left the Principal’s office.

But what was with her attitude? A fleeting suspicion crossed my mind, but I
brushed it off, half in denial.

_No way, right?_

---

In the underground training facility beneath Foranada Castle, a space reserved
for covert practice, three figures stood. One was, of course, me. The second was
Shion, my loyal secretary. And the third—

“\[Hydro Wave\]\!” shouted Garuna, the maid—or rather, Leviathan Luschwe, the
Blue Magic Administrator—who unleashed an advanced water spell at me.

Long ago, she wandered the world freely, but apparently took an interest in me
and knocked on Foranada’s door. I knew her true identity from the start but saw
no hostility, so I shared the information with only a select few and left her
be.

However, things have changed. I initially thought she was just a powerful witch,
but it turns out she’s a Magic Administrator, akin to the Western Demon King. If
there’s even a chance she holds clues about the one destined to lead Caron to
her death, ignoring her isn’t an option.

Fortunately, Garuna—she insisted I call her that—was cooperative. Partly because
I demonstrated the gap in our strength, but also because she seems to enjoy her
current life as a maid. After providing information, she returned to her usual
duties.

So why is she now hurling advanced magic at me?

Simply put, we’re conducting a mock battle with the Western Demon King—Gruewen,
the Gold Magic Administrator—as the hypothetical enemy. Apparently, all Magic
Administrators have immunity to attributes other than their own, making Garuna
the perfect opponent for testing penetration of that resistance.

Her blue hair, tied up in a chignon, glistened faintly with moisture as she
wielded her magic with full force. I’d told her to come at me with killing
intent, and the pressure of her genuine hostility bore down on me.

A massive wave engulfed the vast training field, not just in area but in sheer
volume. A deluge of water surged toward me, intent on crushing me.

An ordinary mage, even with advanced magic, couldn’t produce this level of
power. She’s truly worthy of being called a Magic Administrator.

But this isn’t enough to take me down. Without panicking, I activated a single
spell.

\[Decomposition Analysis\].

Muttered silently in my mind, it took effect instantly. The wave lost its
momentum, collapsing like a failed Jenga tower, breaking apart and dissipating
into water vapor.

\[Decomposition Analysis\] is an application of \[Scan\] and \[Time Stop\]. It
instantly identifies the core of a target and freezes only that core’s time. As
a result, the core is left behind, causing the entire spell to unravel.

The greatest advantage of this technique is that, if desired, it can halt a
target at the atomic level with pinpoint precision. No matter how powerful the
target, it can be dismantled with minimal effort. It’s a spell that eliminates
waste to the extreme.

With the wave gone, visibility cleared. I launched a counterattack at Garuna.

“\[Fifth—” I began, chanting loudly to temper its lethal potential, “—Compress
Cube\]\!”

In an instant, I crushed Garuna.

\[Compress Cube\], which crushes a target from all directions, was cast
fivefold. Even a single instance could easily kill a person, and now five struck
Garuna at once.

That said, she’s not dead. I deliberately chanted aloud, giving her time to
defend.

“Are you _trying_ to kill me\?!” Garuna screamed, standing unscathed.

As expected, she had enough strength to protest loudly while firing ten \[Hydro
Arrows\] at me. I blocked them with a magical barrier.

In return, I fired ten \[Gunshots\].

“Wah, yo, fah\?!” Garuna yelped, dodging frantically.

She hadn’t anticipated a no-look shot, but as expected, opponents with high
magic perception—like her or Marina, who’s contracted with Maimu—are harder to
catch off guard with \[Gunshot\]. I kept firing, experimenting with adjustments,
but she dodged every single one. Tch.

“Hey, come on\!” I called out. “Don’t dodge. This mock battle is to test if my
attacks can pierce a Magic Administrator’s resistance\!”

Exactly. This battle was partly to measure whether my attacks could bypass her
immunity. If she keeps dodging, we can’t test that.

Tearing up, Garuna shouted back, “Don’t be ridiculous\! Your attacks, Zex-sama,
are _obviously_ going to work on us\! I don’t need to take them head-on to know
that—I can tell just by looking\!”

Apparently, she can gauge an attack’s effectiveness just by observing it. She’s
been saying that since the mock battle began.

So why continue the fight?

“I want to confirm the extent of their power with my own eyes,” I said. “So just
give up and let me beat you to a pulp.”

That’s the deal. For an experiment, verifying the impact is essential. Without
seeing it firsthand, I can’t refine it further. No matter how much a Magic
Administrator vouches for it, I won’t budge on this.

“That’s absurd\!” Garuna wailed.

Despite her protests, our mock battle continued.

Several hours later, Garuna lay sprawled on the training field, battered and
bruised.

Well, “battered” might be an exaggeration—being a Magic Administrator made
entirely of magic, her wounds were already healing. As long as their magic
doesn’t run dry, they can keep going indefinitely. That’s something to consider
for future research.

Feeling pleased with the new findings, I was interrupted by Shion, who’d been
observing, as she rushed over.

“Well done, Zex-sama,” she said, handing me a towel and a water bottle. “Here
you are.”

“Thanks,” I said, taking them.

My beloved maid is truly thoughtful. If only she hadn’t tripped on her way here,
she’d be perfect.

I wiped my forehead with the towel and took a sip from the water bottle.
Noticing some dirt on Shion’s cheek, I wiped it off for her.

Garuna, still sprawled with her limbs splayed, suddenly slid to her feet.
Already recovered, huh?

Not fully healed, she raised a protest. “Shion-senpai, could you at least show a
little concern for me? Your junior’s been turned into a tattered rag here\!”

“Garuna, mind your tone\!” Shion snapped.

“Ah, my apologies\!” Garuna said, correcting herself. “But that’s not the
point\! I’m too beaten up to think about manners\!”

“You’re serving Zex-sama’s purpose,” Shion replied coolly. “That should be
enough.”

“She’s a Zex-sama fanatic\!” Garuna groaned. “No reasoning with her\!”

Fanatic, huh? Shion doesn’t blindly agree with me—she’ll call me out when I’m
wrong. But I get what Garuna’s trying to say. I’ve been a bit too dismissive
after dragging her into this experiment, so I decided to cut the teasing short.

---

I cast a spell at Garuna, who was clutching her head in distress.

“\[Mana Transfer\]\!” I declared.

As I used the spell to share my mana, Garuna’s wounds healed before our eyes.
Since Magic Administrators are beings of pure mana, their injuries mend as their
mana increases. Quite convenient in moments like this.

“Haa\?!” Garuna gasped, her eyes wide with disbelief at the phenomenon
unfolding.

“What did you do, Zex-sama\?!” she exclaimed, her voice tinged with shock.

“What do you mean? Just a \[Mana Transfer\],” I replied, tilting my head.

Why was she so surprised? \[Mana Transfer\] is a widely recognized spell, after
all.

“That’s not the point\!” she shouted. “Why is there no conversion loss with your
\[Mana Transfer\]\?!”

“Oh, that,” I said, nodding in understanding.

Mana is a highly individualized energy. Beyond the obvious differences in
attributes, there’s also density and mana quality, which are hereditary traits.
When transferring mana of differing standards, it’s inevitable that not all of
it—100 percent—gets fully absorbed by the recipient. If I recall, traditional
\[Mana Transfer\] has a conversion rate of about 10 percent, right? It’s so
inefficient that it’s only used in emergencies.

But my case is different. I applied the know-how I developed transferring mana
to Noma and refined the \[Mana Transfer\] spell formula. By analyzing the
recipient’s mana quality and converting the transferred mana to near-identical
properties, I eliminated all loss. The trade-off is that the mana manipulation
difficulty skyrocketed.

When I explained this, Garuna’s shoulders trembled with indignation.

“Are you a monster\?!” she cried.

“That’s rude,” I shot back. “I’m human.”

“Exactly, Garuna,” Shion interjected sharply. “The one who’s abandoned humanity
is you, isn’t it?”

“I know, I know\!” Garuna wailed. “But you’ve gotta understand how frustrating
this feels\!”

She collapsed onto all fours, head hanging low.

Sorry, I feel for you, but you’ll have to deal with it.

“Alright, time for the post-match review,” I said, shifting gears.

At that, Garuna shot me a half-lidded glare. “You switch gears way too fast,
don’t you?”

“Time’s finite,” I replied with a shrug.

“Ugh, fine,” she muttered.

She took a swig from the water bottle—prepared by Shion—then straightened her
posture.

“What you want to know, Zex-sama, is the extent to which your attacks can pierce
immunity resistance, right?” she asked, her tone serious.

“Exactly,” I confirmed. “If the Gold Magic Administrator breaks their seal, I’m
the only one who can deal with them. I need to stock up on effective cards.”

Right. If, as in the original story, the Saintess fails to reinforce the Demon
King’s seal, I’d be the only one left to face them head-on.

Originally, I thought light magic users, who aren’t affected by immunity
resistance, would be the best fit—especially Caron, who could likely keep up
with a Demon King’s level.

But that was before we tackled the dungeon. After gathering details from the
dungeon and Garuna, I realized that choice would be catastrophic.

Here’s why: Magic Administrators have the authority to adjust the balance of
their designated color. In practical terms, they can freely grant or strip
magical aptitudes. If we sent a light magic user against the Western Demon King,
the Gold Magic Administrator, they’d have their light magic—their
weapon—stripped away.

That’s just unfair. Rendering other attributes immune while freely controlling
their own attribute? No wonder they boast about being the masters of magic.

As I grumbled about this cheating nonsense, Garuna let out an exasperated sigh.

“I think _you’re_ the unfair one, Zex-sama,” she said. “How do you even break
through immunity resistance? It makes me want to look up the definition of
‘immune’...”

“I’ve been researching countermeasures for a while,” I explained. “Analyzing
that sealed thing gave me a grasp of immunity resistance’s mechanics, and now I
have an excellent sample right in front of me. With all this, it’d be weirder if
I couldn’t do something.”

“Doing something is what’s weird\!” she retorted.

“I agree,” Shion added, nodding.

I got shot down instantly, and even Shion sided with her. Unbelievable.

I don’t get it. Formulating strategies and improving performance against
problems is humanity’s specialty, isn’t it?

Whatever. Garuna’s critique is more important right now.

“So, how’s it actually look?” I asked.

“More than sufficient to kill a Magic Administrator,” she said bluntly.

“How long would it take to defeat one?” I pressed.

“Hmm...” She paused, thinking. “Depends on your fighting style, Zex-sama, but
I’d estimate at least an hour. Grue-chan’s a Gold Magic user, after all.”

“...I’d need to account for their recovery, then,” I muttered.

“Yeah,” she confirmed. “Plus, Grue-chan’s fighting style involves creating
minions to fight for them.”

“So they set up a wall and stay in the backline?” I clarified.

“Pretty much,” she nodded.

“Got it...” I said, rubbing my chin.

Knowing the enemy’s hand is huge, but the problems are piling up. Spending an
hour on a fight, even with efforts to minimize collateral damage, could still
have some negative impact on the world. I’d want to cut it down to thirty
minutes at least.

The challenges are improving penetration power, reducing collateral effects, and
developing tactics against the Gold Magic Administrator based on Garuna’s info.
Worst case, it might be more efficient to let Caron and the others handle the
minions.

As I mulled over these issues, Garuna spoke up hesitantly. “Um, can I head back
now?”

“Hm? Oh, sure, but got somewhere to be?” I asked.

It was already dinner time.

Garuna nodded eagerly. “Yes\! I’m eating with Teria and Maron\!”

“Nice, getting along well, huh?” I said.

Maron aside, I’d tasked Teria with keeping an eye on Garuna. It was just
observation, but it was still espionage, and I’d felt a bit guilty, worried it
might ruin their friendship. Judging by Garuna’s attitude, though, it seems
fine.

“I was shocked to learn Teria was spying on me, but that’s separate,” Garuna
said brightly. “Her treating me like a friend was genuine.”

“Pretty mature of you,” I remarked.

“I have lived a long time\!” she declared, puffing out her chest.

But that bravado quickly crumbled.

“If you’re so mature, maybe take your regular duties more seriously,” Shion said
coolly.

“Th-that’s a different matter, senpai\!” Garuna stammered.

“Mind your tone\!” Shion snapped.

“Y-Yes\!” Garuna squeaked, bowing repeatedly.

She clearly can’t stand up to Shion, shrinking under her authority. There’s
something endearing about her clownish charm that probably keeps their
friendship intact.

“If only all Magic Administrators were like Garuna,” I mused aloud. “Though
that’d probably be its own kind of trouble.”

I gave a wry smile.

Three days after the Principal raised the issue of the first-years’ anomaly, I’d
gathered a rough picture of the situation. Since we built the “Tournament
System” management system, espionage was easier than usual. It’s still just an
overview, so further investigation is needed, but it’s a start.

Among the first-years, thirty-two have been dominating upperclassmen. One is
Tara, but she’s an exception. Apparently, some baron’s son ordered her to be his
concubine, so she challenged him to a “tournament” with that as the stakes and
won.

The real concern is the remaining thirty-one. As the Principal said, they come
from varied regions and factions, with no clear connections based on my initial
probe. The only common thread is that they’re all viscounts or lower, including
some commoners, but that’s a bit too weak to call a solid link.

What’s stranger is that they aren’t all top performers academically. While entry
exam scores aren’t everything, it’s excessive for a first-year B-class student
to defeat a third-year B-class.

Oh, one more thing: among those thirty-one is Kiteros, Eclara’s former master
and Orca’s childhood friend. I don’t want to jump to conclusions, but it’s worth
keeping in mind.

With the broad strokes gathered, I decided to investigate firsthand until more
information comes in.

It’s not complicated—just observe their “tournaments.” Seeing is believing, and
I might spot something.

You might wonder if I’d conveniently catch a “tournament,” but it’s not an
issue. Most of these students are cocky and fight frequently. Plus, I have
access to the management system, so I can check who’s fighting where.

Sure enough, within ten minutes of starting, I hit the jackpot. The opponent was
Nina.

Standing twenty meters ahead of Nina was a first-year male student. He was
lanky, with a sleazy smirk that didn’t scream “formidable fighter.”

The match hadn’t started yet—probably caught them right after the challenge was
accepted, as the countdown hadn’t begun.

“Nina,” I called out.

“...Zex,” she replied, her voice laced with irritation.

Whoa. For someone usually so expressionless to show this much emotion, she must
be seriously pissed. What did this guy say?

The opponent noticed me and flashed a shady grin. “Well, well, if it isn’t Earl
Foranada, the second-year top student\! I’m just a humble merchant’s son, Kobol
Resa Batob. I’ll be challenging you to a ‘tournament’ someday, so please look
forward to it\!”

“Me?” I said, raising an eyebrow.

What a bold declaration. Does he know my reputation? Nah, probably not—or just
scraps of it. Otherwise, he wouldn’t say something so idiotic.

Oblivious to my incredulity, Kobol continued smugly. “I’ve fought A- and B-class
upperclassmen in ‘tournaments,’ but they weren’t challenging at all. So I
thought, ‘Why not aim to conquer the second-years?’”

_You can’t_, I wanted to snap but held back.

“And today, I finally get to challenge a second-year A-1\!” he went on. “Isn’t
it fitting that my first match is against someone with an epithet?”

As Kobol’s smugness grew, Nina’s temple twitched with a visible vein.

...Got it. He’s underestimating her strength, and that’s why she’s furious.
Honestly, anyone would snap at that attitude.

Please don’t tell me all the problem first-years are like this. That’d be
exhausting to watch. At least Kiteros isn’t, so there’s hope. Please let Kobol
be the exception.

Ignoring his self-aggrandizing ramble, I turned to Nina. “Nina, I’ve got a
request.”

“...What?” she said, her brow furrowing slightly, sensing trouble.

She’s sharp.

“Don’t crush him instantly,” I said. “I need you to observe his moves.”

“Why?” she asked, her tone clipped.

“The academy asked me to investigate the first-years’ weird behavior,” I
explained.

“He’s one of them, and you want to know his tricks,” she deduced.

“Exactly,” I confirmed.

She’s quick on the uptake—saves me time.

When I nodded, Nina frowned, deep in thought for about a minute. Reluctantly,
she agreed.

“Fine,” she said. “But you owe me.”

“Thanks,” I said with a grin. “How about a date this weekend?”

“\!” Her eyes lit up. “I’ll do my best\!”

“No, please don’t\!” I said quickly. “I need you to hold back, not go all out\!”

My attempt at gratitude backfired, igniting her enthusiasm. Just don’t
instant-kill him, okay?

As we spoke, the bracelet’s countdown began. The number ten appeared, signaling
the start of the “tournament.”

—_Beep. Please commence combat._

A heavy silence fell. Neither moved.

Nina, needing to observe, was understandably still, but why wasn’t Kobol moving?

Nina and I glanced at him, puzzled.

After about ten seconds, Kobol nodded as if realizing something. “I’m generous,”
he began, likely about to say, I’ll let you go first.

But he never finished.

“...Enough already. Come at me,” Nina snapped, her voice dripping with intense
anger, radiating a pressure that overwhelmed Kobol.

The sheer will of someone who’s broken their limits carries physical force, and
it intimidated him.

“Hii\!” he squeaked, his earlier bravado gone, trembling all over.

Not fainting or wetting himself is something, I guess.

“Damn it, don’t get cocky\!” he spat, shaking.

I raised an eyebrow, mildly impressed. “Hoh.”

To still have pride after that intimidation is surprising. Probably just dull
instincts, but that could be a talent if used right.

Based on his hair, eyes, and mana color, Kobol’s aptitudes are likely earth and
fire. He opened with ten intermediate \[Flare Lances\].

Not bad for a cocky guy. Firing ten intermediate spells at once could indeed
work against second-years.

But it’s not enough for a landslide victory. His opening move alone won’t reveal
much.

Nina seemed to understand, focusing on defense with her weaker earth magic,
forgoing counterattacks.

Kobol’s magical onslaught continued, but nothing got through. Nina blocked
everything.

“Zee... haa...” Kobol panted.

His lanky frame betrayed his low stamina. Barely ten minutes in, he was already
gasping.

It’s hard to believe this guy dominated upperclassmen. It’s not just that Nina’s
too strong—he’s just too weak.

His mana capacity is high, and his ability to cast multiple intermediate spells
is passable, but everything else is a fail. He’s exactly as his entrance exam
suggested.

“Done?” Nina asked, her voice devoid of anger now, replaced by sheer
disappointment at his weakness.

Her words made Kobol flush red, his pride still towering.

He clicked his tongue and muttered, “Guess I have to use _that_. I wanted to
save it in front of Earl Foranada, but...”

What the hell? Talking about holding back despite the massive gap in their
strength? Oh, Nina’s anger is reigniting...

“Take this—my ultimate technique\! \[Lightning Meteor\]\!” Kobol shouted.

He invoked a supreme composite spell of fire, earth, and wind, summoning a
thunder-charged meteor.

Simultaneously, a fifty-meter boulder appeared above Nina’s head, radiating
intense heat and crackling with lightning, descending with a deafening GOGOGOGO
roar.

“Huh?” I blurted.

“Eh?” Nina echoed.

We both let out stunned noises.

Sure, it’s shocking that a mage of Kobol’s caliber could cast a supreme
composite spell, but that’s not the biggest issue. The real problem is how he
cast \[Lightning Meteor\].

Let’s confirm: \[Lightning Meteor\] is a composite of fire, earth, and wind.
Kobol, however, only has aptitudes for fire and earth. No matter his skill,
there’s no way he should be able to use this spell. What he did defies the laws
of the world.

“...No, wait,” I muttered.

I looked at Kobol again with \[Magic Vision\]. His mana now carried traces of
wind, which weren’t there before.

Whatever the cause, there’s a trick here. It’s highly likely tied to the
first-years’ anomaly.

“Nina, that’s enough,” I said.

“Got it,” she replied.

We got the information we needed. Time to end this farce.

Right after Nina’s response, the falling meteor shattered into pinky-sized
fragments and vanished. Almost simultaneously, Kobol’s HP gauge hit zero. Nina
had sliced through everything in an instant.

_—“Tournament” concluded._

The bracelet signaled the match’s end, and the barrier dissipated.

“Got him,” I said.

I knocked out the dazed Kobol, still reeling from his ultimate technique’s
failure, and secured him with \[Phase Concealment\].

I’ll hand him over to the covert team for interrogation.

Wonder what secrets he’ll spill.

After Nina’s “tournament” with Kobol, I continued investigating the problematic
first-years.

To cut to the chase, twenty-seven of the thirty-one were using magic of
aptitudes they shouldn’t have. It’s highly likely they’re involved in some
incident, so we detained them promptly. Fortunately, their low social status
meant the academy’s behind-the-scenes maneuvering kept the situation from
escalating into a scandal.

Interrogating the detained first-years revealed that the cause of their expanded
magical aptitudes was a drug. We couldn’t obtain the substance itself, but they
unanimously claimed, “A merchant from the city-state cluster gave it to us.”
There’s likely no lie there, though the merchant’s identity is dubious.

Additionally, thanks to humane experiments, we grasped the drug’s general
properties despite not having a sample. They call it “Outer.”

“Outer” has two main effects.

The first is obvious: it randomly grants an additional magical aptitude. Based
on their mana flow, the ability lies dormant normally but manifests when the
user intends to use it. In some cases, this causes slight changes in hair or eye
color.

The second is an increase in mana capacity. I’d felt something off about their
above-average mana levels, and it turns out this was also due to the drug. I
don’t know their original capacities, but according to their self-reports, it
doubled or tripled. For a single dose to cause such a surge is, frankly,
abnormal.

As for side effects, there are two: the need to periodically discharge mana to
avoid overloading, and an unnatural sense of euphoria.

Pretty mild drawbacks for such potent effects, right? Well, constant euphoria is
a significant downside, I suppose.

By the way, these side effects were uncovered through our investigation. All
twenty-seven students were completely unbothered by them. That’s just too
empty-headed—I was floored by their nonchalance.

Since this is based on current findings, other side effects might emerge later.
For instance, it wouldn’t be surprising if it’s “shortening their lifespan” or
“borrowing future mana.” In fact, the former is almost certain.

When they use mana, it places an extreme burden on their bodies. Since the side
effect requires them to discharge mana regularly, they’re essentially guaranteed
to shave off their own lifespans.

Foranada’s overall assessment of “Outer” is clear: it’s undoubtedly a prohibited
drug. This needs to be reported to the state. After all, they acquired “Outer”
before enrollment, back in their hometowns.

So, I sent a report to First Prince Wiemlay, and “Outer” was swiftly designated
a prohibited substance. Crackdowns within the kingdom are already underway.

For now, it’s a waiting game. If new “Outer” users appear, the state will handle
them.

As for the twenty-seven first-years, they’ll remain under observation. I’ve
coordinated with Wiemlay, and they’ll be transferred to a surveillance hospital
when the time comes—the same one we sent Laurel to, used for criminals, where
further research on “Outer” will be conducted.

“...And that’s the gist of it,” I said, leaning back on the sofa.

“...I see,” the Principal replied softly.

We were face-to-face in the Principal’s office again, this time for me to report
on the “Outer” incident.

Having heard the full account, she looked visibly dejected. She’d received
regular updates, but having the facts laid bare seemed to hit her hard.

Just to clarify, the Principal bears almost no blame here. The students took
“Outer” outside the academy, so she couldn’t have prevented it. Plus, as a new
drug, early detection was near impossible.

If anything, the damage was minimized. Without the “Tournament System,” someone
might’ve been killed in a random attack, driven by the need to discharge
overflowing mana.

Still, no amount of reasoning would lift her spirits. She’s a born educator.
It’s not about her responsibility—she’s pained by her inability to save
promising students. I could tell that much without reading her emotions. Though
we’ve known each other for less than a year, dealing with incidents together has
built enough of a rapport to understand her character. ...Not that I wanted to
know about her perversions, mind you.

I spoke gently, softening my tone. “I won’t tell you not to feel down, but this
wasn’t your fault. You can’t control what kids do before they enroll. That’s on
their families.”

If I had to point fingers, the families of the “Outer” users were the most
negligent. Letting their kids interact with a shady “merchant from the
city-state cluster”? That’s a massive failure, especially for noble houses.

That’s probably why the “Outer” users were all viscounts or lower. Lower ranks
mean lower-quality guards. Commoners don’t even have guards. To the merchant,
they were perfect marks.

“No one got hurt, and—sorry for them—only twenty-seven out of about 130,000
students were affected. The academy’s barely been impacted,” I added.

“Even so, I couldn’t protect those twenty-seven students...” she murmured, her
voice heavy with guilt.

“Then consider this: you protected the other 400,000-plus,” I countered.

Sure, we couldn’t save a small handful, but the vast majority were spared.
That’s because we detected the first-years’ anomaly early and acted. Her
decision to take it seriously and enlist Foranada was sharp.

“If they’d gotten ‘Outer’ within the academy, that’d be on you,” I continued.
“But that’s not the case. You might not feel proud, and you’ll probably regret
it, but there’s no need to put yourself down. That’ll just make the protected
students anxious. You’re the head of this academy, after all.”

The leader of an organization can’t show weakness. They have to carry themselves
with confidence. Emotions at the top ripple down to the bottom.

Meeting my direct gaze, the Principal flinched slightly.

“Don’t you have a gentler way to console someone?” she said, her tone a mix of
exasperation and vulnerability.

I knew it wasn’t the kindest thing to say to someone feeling low, but still.
“You’re not so fragile that this’d break you, right?” I said, smirking.

She may look like a ten-year-old girl, but she’s a witch who’s lived for
centuries. She stood up to my baptism; I’d be troubled if this made her buckle.

She gave a wry smile. “That’s true, but...”

“If you need to vent, I’ll listen a bit,” I offered. “I’ve already seen your
pathetic side, after all.”

I still remember her groveling during our first meeting. A master of first-class
kowtowing, worthy of a title.

I meant it as a playful jab to lighten the serious mood.

But her reaction was odd.

“Y-Yes, you’re right,” she said, blushing and averting her gaze. “With you, I
feel like I can show my weaker side.”

Huh? Isn’t this where she’s supposed to snap, “_Don’t bring that up_\!” Why’s
she acting all maidenly?

The sudden sweet atmosphere left me bewildered.

There’s no... romantic emotion in her. Just pure fondness. So, what—her witch
days were spent in solitude, and as Principal, she’s always been on the front
lines, with no one to lean on? Then I, a powerhouse, show up, and she starts
letting her guard down?

How cliché, I thought, wondering if it was rude to think so.

If this keeps up, she might actually fall for me. But cutting ties or changing
course now would be tough...

“What happens, happens,” I muttered under my breath.

The die’s already cast. Rather than fretting, I should face the future head-on.
Overthinking just wears you out.

My tolerance for this kind of mess might be the thing I’ve honed most since
reincarnating.

Recalling past romantic entanglements, I let out a resigned, distant sigh.
